The first trained NetSilcon-64x6 model drives a rover around obstacles. It does that well, but it has no idea where it is going. Put it in a maze and it wanders: in 80 test mazes it found the exit 38 times, mostly by luck.

So the same network shape got a second job. **Same chip, same 16 LiDAR inputs, same two outputs. Only the model file changed.**

| Test mazes, never seen in training | Maze model | Rover model |
|---|---|---|
| 4 × 4 cells, 1.6 m corridors | 20 / 20 | 12 / 20 |
| 6 × 6 cells, 1.6 m corridors | 19 / 20 | 12 / 20 |
| 8 × 8 cells, 1.6 m corridors | 18 / 20 | 6 / 20 |
| 6 × 6 cells, 1.2 m corridors | 15 / 20 | 8 / 20 |
| **All 80 mazes** | **72 / 80, 0 crashes** | 38 / 80, 1 crash |

## The trick: keep one hand on the wall

In a maze with exactly one path between any two places, there is an old rule that always works: keep your right hand on the wall and keep walking. You will visit every corner of the maze eventually, including the exit. No map, no memory.

For a car-like rover that becomes: head for the right-most open direction. A corridor on the right? Take it. If not, go straight; if not, turn left. At a dead end, stop, and a small fixed 3-point-turn helper turns the rover around.

## Three attempts, one lesson

The first teacher was clever: it knew the maze map and the rover's exact position, and chose each turn from that. The network copied it badly, solving only 4 of 20 small mazes. Removing the extra memory inputs made little difference (6 of 20).

The reason: the network cannot see the map or its position. Two moments that look exactly the same in the LiDAR could carry different teacher decisions, so the network learned a blurred average of them.

What worked was a teacher that decides from **the same 16 distances the network sees**. Then the network can learn it exactly, and it did: 72 of 80 mazes, the same as its teacher, and slightly better in the narrow corridors (15 against 12).

*A teacher is only useful if the student can see what the teacher sees.*

## Honest limits

- **Simulation only.** The model's integer maths was checked in the Verilog simulation (1,200 of 1,200 golden vectors match), but it has not driven with the real board yet.
- **It explores, it doesn't plan.** It does not find the shortest route, and big mazes take a few minutes.
- **Dead ends need the helper.** The 3-point turn is fixed logic beside the network, as in the rover tests.
- **8 of 80 mazes timed out**, 5 of them in the narrow 1.2 m corridors, where the rover barely fits through a turn.

## Try it

The [maze simulator](/playground/#maze) runs the real model file in your browser with the chip's integer maths. Generate a new maze, switch between the maze model and the rover model, and watch the difference. Its physics follows the Python simulator step for step: on test drives the two traced the same path to within a hundredth of a millimetre.
